People tell chatbots things they wouldn't tell anyone else: health worries, relationship problems, work secrets, details about friends and family. We usually ask whether the AI company will **protect** that data. A recent BBC Future article asks a harder question: *what happens to it when the company goes bust?*

The trigger is the bankruptcy of **Character.ai**, and the answer should change how both users and builders think about AI data.

## What happened

- **Character Technologies**, the company behind Character.ai, filed for **Chapter 11 bankruptcy** in October 2026 and is selling assets under court supervision.
- The lead deal is a reported **$20 million** agreement with **Disney** for some of its assets — and court filings say **user data is not part of that deal**.
- But the filings also leave the door open to selling user data **to other buyers**. At an early hearing, the company's lawyer said no one had yet bid for the user data.
- The service has around **163 million** current and past accounts, including many young users.
- The **US Department of Justice** has argued that any sale including consumer data shouldn't be approved without a **consumer privacy ombudsman**, and a coalition of state attorneys general has asked for protections such as age verification, limits on training AI with private conversations, and a chance for users to access and delete their data before any sale.

As privacy expert **Calli Schroeder** of the Electronic Privacy Information Center told the BBC, people share more with personified bots — and users also share information about **people who never signed up**.

## Why bankruptcy is different

Privacy policies make promises, but most include an exception for **mergers, acquisitions and asset sales**. In bankruptcy, customer data becomes an **asset** that can be sold to pay creditors. Courts and regulators can attach conditions, and a buyer may have to honour the original policy — but if that policy already allowed broad uses such as model training or advertising profiles, those uses can travel with the data. Law professor **Melissa Jacoby** told the BBC that without stronger US privacy law, there is only so much regulators can do.

Data sales in bankruptcy are not new. What's new is the **intimacy** of the data: not a purchase history, but years of private conversations.

## What users can do

- **Treat chatbots like a public diary.** Don't share passwords, ID numbers, medical records or other people's private details.
- **Turn off training on your chats** where the setting exists, and use temporary or incognito chat modes for sensitive topics.
- **Delete what you don't need.** Old conversations, unused accounts and abandoned apps are risk with no benefit. Character.ai published deletion instructions with its bankruptcy announcement.
- **Prefer providers with clear retention limits** and a business model that doesn't depend on your data.

## What builders should do

If you're building an AI product, design as if your company's data could one day be sold — because it could.

**1. Collect and keep less.** Store conversations only as long as they serve the user, with automatic deletion by default. Data you've deleted can't be sold.

**2. Separate identity from content.** Keep user accounts and conversation content in separate stores, with conversation data encrypted and keys managed separately.

**3. Make training opt-in.** Don't train on private conversations without explicit, informed consent — and record that consent.

**4. Write a policy that survives a sale.** Commit that personal conversation data will be **deleted rather than transferred** if the company is sold or wound down, unless users opt in to the transfer.

**5. Give users real controls.** Simple export and one-click deletion, applied everywhere — backups and logs included.

**6. Protect minors by design.** Age assurance, stricter defaults and shorter retention for younger users.

## The bottom line

The AI bubble debate is usually about valuations. Character.ai shows the human side: when an AI company fails, **the most valuable thing left may be its users' secrets**. Users should share less and delete more. Builders should minimise, encrypt and commit to deletion — so that if the business fails, people's private lives don't end up on the auction block.
